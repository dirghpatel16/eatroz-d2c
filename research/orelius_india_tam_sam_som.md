# Orelius India TAM, SAM and SOM

## Executive conclusion

Orelius should not use the broad Indian nutraceutical market as its addressable market in an investor pitch. Those figures include functional foods, beverages, clinical nutrition, traditional supplements, and channels that Orelius will not serve at launch. They demonstrate category momentum, not attainable demand for a premium daily gummy.

The decision-ready market is the narrower Indian vitamins, minerals and supplements (VMS) space, with a further filter for urban, digitally reachable consumers willing to make daily nutrition a paid habit. On that basis, the recommended sizing frame is:

| Layer | Orelius definition | Value | Status |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Broad category context | India dietary supplements / nutraceuticals | US$5.8B–38.8B in 2025 across incompatible report definitions | Third-party estimate; context only |
| TAM | Indian VMS spend that daily nutrition may compete for | **less than ₹10,000 Cr** | Founder operating estimate; conservative decision anchor |
| SAM | Premium, digitally reachable daily-nutrition buyers within VMS | **₹800–1,500 Cr** | Planning range; assumption-led filter of TAM |
| SOM, years 1–3 | Revenue justified by the local cohort model | **~₹5.5–20.8 Cr annualised run-rate** | Model scenario, not forecast |
| India-first scaling objective | Durable next-stage proof before institutional capital | **₹20–50 Cr annualised run-rate** | Recommendation, not forecast |

The model says the category is worth entering only if Orelius creates repeat behaviour. A generic Grüns-style clone competes against existing gummies on price, flavour and paid advertising. An India-adapted daily-nutrition brand can compete on a different basis: lower daily friction, transparent amounts, visible quality evidence, and a recurring 28-day habit.

## What the market reports do—and do not—say

Current third-party reports agree on growth but disagree sharply on market size because they define the category differently.

| Source | Reported market | Use in this analysis | Limitation |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Grand View Research, India dietary supplements | US$5.81B in 2025; US$15.60B by 2033 | Directional evidence that supplements are a material category | Vendor estimate; not a measure of D2C gummies or Orelius buyers |
| Grand View Research, India nutraceuticals | US$38.77B in 2025; 10.3% CAGR to 2033 | Demonstrates broad preventative-health/functional-food momentum | Functional food and beverage made up 76.4% of the cited market; too broad for Orelius |
| IMARC, India functional gummies | US$5.6B in 2025; 10.78% CAGR to 2034 | Directional confirmation that convenient formats are expanding | Definition and underlying channel mix are not sufficiently transparent to use as Orelius revenue TAM |
| Revant Bhate, Mosaic Wellness | VMS is “sub ₹10,000 crore” | Conservative operating anchor for the category Orelius actually competes in | Founder statement, not a syndicated measurement study |

The correct investor language is: **Indian nutrition and supplement demand is growing at a double-digit reported rate, but Orelius sizes its opportunity from the narrower VMS pool and from demonstrated customer economics—not by claiming a share of every nutraceutical rupee.**

## Market map: Indian brands prove demand but also cap differentiation

| Brand / adjacent proof point | Current evidence | What it proves | Implication for Orelius |
|---|---|---|---|
| Man Matters | 60-gummy men’s multivitamin at ₹749; two gummies/day; pectin and FOS stated | Men will buy a convenient daily vitamin gummy | A generic multivitamin is already a commodity proposition |
| Be Bodywise | Women’s multivitamin gummy at ₹699; one/day; no added refined sugar stated | Low/no-added-sugar positioning is already expected | Orelius cannot treat sugar reduction as its sole moat |
| Kapiva | Shilajit gummy: ₹999 for 60; one/day; states an NABL report | Premium pricing can work with a specific benefit and quality communication | Quality proof and a clear job-to-be-done support price discipline |
| Siens by Dabur | Dabur launched a premium D2C nutraceutical brand and beauty gummy | Large incumbents can enter focused gummy propositions quickly | Format alone is not defensible |
| Little Joys | Age-specific formats and public quality-report communication | Trust can be designed into the product experience | QR-linked finished-product evidence is a practical differentiation opportunity |
| Supply6 | Reported ₹75 Cr ARR and ₹48 Cr Series A led by Unilever Ventures in 2026 for foundational nutrition | Indian demand exists for convenient, comprehensive nutrition | The concept travels; gummies are not required for the concept to work |
| Power Gummies | Reported FY25 revenue of ₹3.5 Cr after a steep decline; Panacea Biotec later disclosed acquisition of its brand/IP assets | A gummy-first brand can fail despite category awareness | Avoid a format-only strategy, excessive paid acquisition, and weak retention |

This is a competitive market, not an empty white space. The opening is not “India has no gummies.” The opening is a credible, daily foundational-nutrition ritual for adults that combines taste, transparent formulation, and proof of finished-product quality.

## TAM

### Recommended TAM: less than ₹10,000 Cr

For planning, TAM is the annual Indian VMS spend that a daily adult nutrition gummy could plausibly compete for. Mosaic Wellness founder Revant Bhate described the total VMS market as below ₹10,000 crore in a founder discussion. This is a substantially more decision-useful anchor than broad nutraceutical forecasts because it excludes much of the functional-food and beverage spend that Orelius cannot win at launch.

This is still not “Orelius revenue waiting to be captured.” It contains prescription-adjacent products, price-sensitive customers, traditional supplements, products for other life stages, and offline buyers who may never switch to a D2C gummy.

### Why broad market reports remain secondary

The US$38.77B nutraceutical figure includes a functional-food-and-beverage share of 76.4%. The US$5.81B dietary-supplement figure is nearer to the relevant category but still includes many forms, needs, and channels. The US$5.6B functional-gummy estimate has a category definition too broad to treat as a direct market for an adult daily superfood pack. These reports help establish momentum; they do not establish willingness to pay ₹699–999 every month for Orelius.

## SAM

### Recommended SAM: ₹800–1,500 Cr

SAM is the portion of the VMS pool that Orelius can plausibly serve in its India-first phase: urban, English/Hinglish digital consumers, initially 18–35, who have health intent, can buy online, and are willing to pay for a monthly daily-nutrition routine.

The range uses an explicit planning filter of roughly 8–15% of the conservative ₹10,000 Cr VMS anchor. It is a scenario assumption, not a published statistic. It reflects the fact that Orelius is excluding children, medically directed products, mass-value offline buyers, protein-led supplementation, and consumers who only make occasional immunity/beauty purchases.

This range should be replaced after six months with observed customer data: city mix, age, household income proxy, acquisition source, willingness to subscribe, reorder behaviour, and actual monthly spend. Until then, it is better to show a transparent range than a false precision number.

### Price and customer-equivalent check

At a ₹699 28-day pack, a 12-order customer produces ₹8,388 of annual gross revenue before discounts and returns.

| Scenario | Revenue | Full-year ₹699 customer equivalents | Share of ₹800–1,500 Cr SAM |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Moderate self-funded model, month-36 annualised run-rate | ₹5.5 Cr | ~65,600 | ~0.37–0.69% |
| Sanode-backed optimistic model, month-36 annualised run-rate | ₹20.8 Cr | ~247,975 | ~1.39–2.60% |
| Illustrative ₹50 Cr next-stage objective | ₹50 Cr | ~595,664 | ~3.33–6.25% |

This arithmetic is intentionally revealing. A ₹5–20 Cr business does not require a large share of the assumed SAM if customers buy every month. But if the average adult customer buys only two packs per year—as discussed by Aarti Gill—those same revenue levels require approximately 393,000 to 1.49 million unique annual buyers. Therefore retention, not first-order reach, determines whether the business is scalable.

## SOM

### Recommended three-year SOM: ₹5.5–20.8 Cr annualised run-rate

SOM is not calculated by assigning Orelius a generic percentage of market share. It is calculated from the local cohort model using explicit order economics, acquisition costs, marketing reinvestment, and operating expenses.

| Model case | Committed capital | Month-36 annualised run-rate | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Lean bootstrap | ₹50L | ~₹1.1 Cr; cash-negative | Insufficient runway for product, retention, and channel proof |
| Moderate self-funded | ₹2 Cr | ~₹5.5 Cr | Credible proof-of-model path, assuming the modelled retention curve |
| Sanode-backed self-funded | ₹5 Cr | ~₹20.8 Cr | Upside scenario, not a base forecast |
| India repeat stress test | ₹5 Cr | ~₹1.45 Cr | Outcome when adult repeat resembles two boxes/year |

The right investor conclusion is not that Orelius will reach ₹20.8 Cr. It is that a ₹2–5 Cr India-first validation budget can generate decision-quality proof; a much larger India outcome requires demonstrated repeat and then external growth capital.

## Video and founder evidence

The stored long-form video work changes how Orelius should be built.

| Speaker / case | Evidence | Operating lesson for Orelius |
|---|---|---|
| Chad Janis, Grüns | Grüns built a daily-pack format around a recurring habit; the retained research records D2C profitability around month 14 and a rule of at least 3x fully-burdened LTV:CAC on delivered gross profit | Copy the discipline and format insight, not the US formula, price point, or scale assumptions |
| Aarti Gill, OZiva | She described weak retention in adult nutrition and said scaled brands had seen a maximum of two boxes per year | Do not fund scale on first-order conversion; set a Month-2/3 repeat gate before increasing spend |
| Revant Bhate, Mosaic Wellness | He described VMS as sub ₹10,000 Cr and cautioned that consumer-health demand is not yet an automatic habit | Use a narrow India case; do not cite huge broad-market reports as validation |
| Satiya Zaveri, Plix | He illustrated how CAC can rise from ₹500 to ₹2,000 when many companies chase the same category | Creator, referral, search, CRM, and product trust must reduce paid-social dependency |

## Required business-model gates

The following are proposed internal controls, not market facts:

| Gate | Proposed threshold | Reason |
|---|---:|---|
| Month-2/3 repeat | ≥35% | First useful evidence that a monthly habit may be forming |
| CAC | ≤₹500; target ≤₹400 in early validation | Current model gives ~₹395 contribution per order before marketing |
| Prepaid mix | ≥70% | Protects cash conversion and reduces COD losses / RTO exposure |
| LTV:CAC | ≥3x on delivered gross contribution | Mirrors the economic discipline documented for Grüns |
| Paid-media concentration | No single paid platform above 50% of new customers | Limits CAC shocks as competitors copy creative |
| Product evidence | Lot-specific finished-product assay, microbiology, contaminants and stability evidence before claims | Trust and compliance are part of the product, not a later marketing layer |

If those gates do not hold, the correct response is to improve formulation, positioning, onboarding, or price architecture—not simply buy more traffic.

## Investment implication

Orelius can become a profitable and scalable Indian business if it is managed as a retained-revenue nutrition company. The initial goal is not a large national launch. It is to prove that a meaningful subset of Indian customers will choose a credible daily nutrition pack every 28 days without continuous discounting.

The underwriting conclusion remains:

- A literal Grüns copy: low probability of durable success because Indian price ceilings, direct competitors, and low adult-nutrition repeat work against it.
- An India-adapted, evidence-first Orelius: viable validation opportunity, conditional on repeat and CAC gates.
- A ₹5–20 Cr annualised run-rate by year 3: model-supported range with ₹2–5 Cr committed capital and favourable retention.
- A ₹50 Cr+ outcome: requires proven cohorts, channel expansion, and likely institutional funding; it should not be promised from an India-first self-funded launch.

## Limitations and next validation work

- Third-party market reports use incompatible definitions and should never be summed.
- The SAM is a transparent planning range, not a survey-derived estimate.
- Market size cannot prove a monthly habit, product efficacy, retention, or margin.
- Product formula, FSSAI category/claims, GST classification, trademark clearance, and manufacturing certifications remain separate diligence workstreams.
- Before investor circulation, replace planning assumptions with first-party cohort data from the first 500–1,000 paid customers.
