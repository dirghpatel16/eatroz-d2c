### **1. Executive Summary & Context**
Fitness personality and commentator James Smith sent multiple market-leading brands of creatine gummies to an independent third-party laboratory (**Eurofins Scientific**) to test whether they actually contained the advertised amount of creatine monohydrate.

This follow-up investigation addresses legal threats (cease-and-desist letters) from supplement companies and exposes a widespread industry issue where several popular brands contain **less than 2% to 3%** of the claimed creatine dose, effectively selling regular candy at exorbitant markups.

### **4. The "PUSH Gummies" Defense Controversy**

PUSH Gummies (one of the largest sellers in Australia and the UK) issued a social media statement claiming that standard lab tests (HPLC) are invalid for **pectin-based (vegan) gummies**, asserting that pectin binds to creatine and causes testing methods to falsely report zero creatine.

**James Smith’s Refutation:**
1. **Redacted Reports:** PUSH published Eurofins lab sheets on their website with redacted QR codes, which legally invalidates the report’s verification chain.
2. **Methodology Contradiction:** Other tested brands using **pectin** as their main gelling agent (*Create*, *Known Nutrition*) tested overwhelmingly positive for creatine using the exact same standard testing procedures. If pectin hid creatine from standard lab equipment, no pectin gummy would test positive.

---

### **5. James Smith’s Verdict & Broader Industry Implications**

* **Sensory / Taste Test:** James notes that real creatine powder leaves a distinct, slightly gritty texture in gummies (as seen in *Create* or *WellBoost*). Products like PUSH taste purely like smooth, sugary candy because there is no raw powder present inside them.
* **The Dilemma for Brands:** James asserts there are only two possibilities for brands selling zero-creatine gummies:
  1. **Intentional Fraud:** They knowingly manufacture plain candy with huge profit margins and market them as supplements.
  2. **Gross Negligence:** They buy cheap white-label products from untrustworthy manufacturers without conducting basic third-party batch testing.
* **Advice to Consumers:** Creatine gummies are disproportionately expensive ($50–$60 per month) compared to standard creatine monohydrate powder ($15–$25 for several months). Given the widespread mislabeling, lack of regulatory oversight, and high risk of receiving fake products, consumers should remain extremely cautious of gummy supplements and stick to verified brands or standard unflavored powders.
